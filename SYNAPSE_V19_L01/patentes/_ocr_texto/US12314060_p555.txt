As autonomous and semi-autonomous systems (generally
referred to as autonomous-oriented systems) become more
prevalent, the interaction among and between such systems
will require varying degrees of management. This manage-
ment may range from high-level coordination to direct 35 
facilitation and/or direct control. For example, an autono-
mous-oriented system may rely on or interact with one or
more other autonomous-oriented systems. To illustrate, one
autonomous-oriented system may be assigned or otherwise
assume a principal task. This autonomous-oriented system 40
may be referred to as a primary autonomous-oriented (PAO)
system.
In various implementations, the principal task can be
divided into subtasks. These subtasks may be controlled by
or utilize one or more secondary autonomous-oriented 45
(SAO) systems. In a simple configuration,this could mean
that one SAO system is responsible for each subtask. In
other configurations, one SAO system could be responsible
for multiple subtasks. In various implementations, a single
subtask may be handled by a set of SAO systems. 50
In a situation where the principal task has many subtasks,
the PAO system therefore has the responsibility to manage
some aspect of the SAO systems. For instance, the PAO
system may communicate information to an SAO system to
initiate functionality of the SAO system to complete the 55
subtask (for example, by providing an initial input to the
SAO system). In other situations, the PAO system leverages
SAO systems that are generating data or information at some
ongoing frequency to manage a particular subtask. In this
situation, the PAO system may be configured to access or to 60
request some portion of the information generated by the
ongoing SAO system. As an example, the primary task, such
as a logistics task, may have a subtask to determine the
current weather or a prediction of the weather at a future
date. Here, there may be an SAO system (e.g., operated by 65
a third party) that determines weather information on an
ongoing basis. Since such an SAO system already exists in

Depending on the extent of the primary task, a subtask
may be further nestedthat is, much like a primary task, a
subtask could be divided into more tasks that can each be
controlled by one or more SAO systems. Dividing a primary
task into multiple levels of subtasks (e.g., a first level of
subtasks, a second level of sub-subtasks, a third level of
sub-sub-subtasks, etc.) may be advantageous for autono-
mous/semi-autonomous systems. One example and non-
limiting advantage in various implementations is parallel
processing. With a primary task divided into tasks and levels
of tasks, the execution of the primary task may be distributed
across a greater number of computing resources (e.g., com-
puter resources underlying SAO systems). Leveraging indi-
vidual systems allows each system to execute their task(s)
autonomously such that a certain set of tasks may be
executed simultaneously. This parallel approach may there-
fore reduce the processing time for the entirety of the
primary task because, to the extent logistically and techno-
logically feasible, subtasks are completed at overlapping
times rather than sequentially.
While distributing executable tasks across a vast number
of resources (e.g., SAO systems) may result in efficient time
for processing, this efficiency has a cost in terms of coor-
dination effort and complexity. The coordination between
autonomous-oriented systems may be difficult. For example,
knowing what resources to make accessible to autonomous-
oriented systems or knowing how to configure a set of
autonomous-oriented systems for a primary task can dis-
suade even those who could ultimately derive value from
coordinating such implementation. To further complicate the
matter, one or more autonomous-oriented systems may
 already be deployed performing functions that are indepen-
dent of the primary task. Here, it may be beneficial to utilize
these deployed autonomous-oriented systems, but configur-
with the primary task can present compatibility issues.
One environment that is ripe for autonomous-oriented
systems management is an organization or enterprise. A
business organization that functions as an enterprise can
have a lot of moving pieces with a particular overarching
purpose, goal, or objective. For instance, a business purpose
may be to produce a particular product line. To achieve its
purpose, the enterprise may have a structure that includes
various business units, such as divisions, departments, job
roles, facilities, etc. An enterprise may span multiple busi-
ness sectors and may therefore have business units, such as
divisions, that can be dedicated to a particular business
sector.
Although the structure of an enterprise in terms of its
business units may differ, there are organizational roles that
may be common across many enterprises. A corporate entity
typically has tax obligations, human resources to manage,
and financial and contractual obligations. From this perspec-
tive, enterprise-style businesses tend to have, among others,
an accounting department for financial and tax matters, a
sales department to initiate contractual obligations, a human
resources department to manage the human resources, a
legal department to oversee contracts and other legal mat-
ters, etc. The business units of an enterprise can also differ
depending on the type of company or activity that a par-
ticular portion of the company (i.e., division) performs. As
