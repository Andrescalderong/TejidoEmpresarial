One approach to address some of the issues with resource
management at the enterprise level is to design a control
architecture based on an enterprise's value chain.A value 5o
chain can refer to a set of connections between organiza-
tions, business units, or individuals associated with these
entities. In a value chain, the connections are based on
interactions that promote a measure of value. For example,
an enterprise value chain is a set of connections that promote 55
value for the overarching purpose, goal, or objective of the
enterprise. For a commercial enterprise, the value chain may
generally be constructed according to a set of relationships
that produce and/or increase owner/shareholder value as the
measure of value.
In some configurations, value chains may be further
classified into value chain networks. Similar to a value chain
that includes a set of connections, the set of connections for
a value chain network includes a group of entities with one
or more similar attributes (e.g-, similar asset management
responsibilities/controls). This group may be considered a
“network"' of a value chain because, for example, the inter-

In various implementations, an enterprise may have mul-
tiple value chain networks. This could mean that one value
chain network may refer to an operations business unit of the
enterprise (i.e, an operations value chain network). Another
value chain network may be a marketing and sales business
unit of the enterprise (i.e., a marketing and sales value chain
network). Yet another value chain network may be for a
logistics business unit of the enterprise (i.e, a logistics value
chain network). In this sense, a value chain network for a
commercial business may be an economic ecosystem where
each member of the network relies on other members of the
network to promote value. The members of these networks
may be internal members of the enterprise (e.g., teams or
individual employees) or external members (e.g., custom-
ers).
