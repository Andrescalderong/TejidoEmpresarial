In some implementations, there may be multiple sets of
service building blocks that are capable of performing a
given activity 21332', especially in a configuration that may
be dictated by CSSs. When this is the case, an SOS, such as
a primary system 21100', can (as noted above) identify the
candidate sets of service building blocks that are capable of
performing an activity 21332' and determine an optimal set
from the candidate sets. In some examples, the optimal set
of service building blocks is determined by an optimization
algorithm that weighs one or more different importance
factors. Some examples of importance factors include con-
sumption of computing resources, duty cycle, response time,
estimated time to complete the activity 21332', amount of
computing resources consumed by internal resources in
relation to third-party resource consumption, training or
initiation time, quality metrics, inventory metrics/thresh-
olds, human resource expenditure, overhead, cost, raw mate-
rial consumption, operational capacity, and resource utiliza-
tion.
Although FIG. 176D illustrates twenty-four service build-
ing blocks, there may be a greater or a fewer number of
service building blocks depending on the services available
to an enterprise. In some examples, the number of service
building blocks is contingent upon the number of services
that systems of the enterprise are capable of performing.
Meaning that, an enterprise with five systems capable of
performing seven service(s)/task(s) may have seven service
building blocks such that the enterprise can deploy a con-
figured instance of some set/subset of the seven service
blocks. With this type of structure, a management stack
21300' can focus on optimizing a configuration for a com-
bination of services rather than trying to optimize or con-
figure each service individually.
